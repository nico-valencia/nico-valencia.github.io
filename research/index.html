Seismic Anisotropy & Shear-Wave Splitting

My primary research focuses on measuring azimuthal anisotropy in the upper mantle of the Southwest Pacific. Seismic anisotropy—the directional dependence of seismic wave velocity—results from the alignment of olivine crystals due to mantle flow and deformation. By measuring how shear waves split into fast and slow components, we can infer patterns of mantle flow beneath the surface.

Traditional SKS splitting methods face challenges in the Southwest Pacific due to poor azimuthal coverage and complex anisotropic structure. I develop methods that combine SKS phases with direct S waves from deep earthquakes, using splitting intensity to extract robust measurements from challenging data. This work contributes constraints for full-waveform azimuthal anisotropy tomography of the region.

I am also developing methods to measure and model multi-layer anisotropy using splitting intensity, extending theoretical expressions to account for multiple anisotropic layers in the upper mantle.

Ocean-Bottom Seismology

Beginning in Fall 2026, my PhD research at UC Santa Barbara will focus on ocean-bottom seismology. Most seismometers are deployed on land, leaving oceanic regions poorly sampled. Ocean-bottom seismometers (OBS) allow us to record seismic waves in these data-sparse regions, providing new constraints on Earth's interior structure.

My research will involve working with new OBS datasets to study seismic structure beneath oceanic regions. This section will be updated as my PhD research develops.

Antarctic Ice Sheet Structure

During a summer research experience at Brown University, I used Ps receiver functions to study Antarctic ice sheet properties. Seismic waves convert from P to S at sharp velocity contrasts—including the base of the ice sheet—allowing us to measure ice thickness and internal structure from seismic data.

At high frequencies, Ps receiver functions can resolve ice thickness changes of ~200 m and velocity variations of ~5%. Thin low-velocity layers at the base of the ice (possibly liquid water or wet sediments) produce distinctive signatures in the receiver functions.

For stations operating over decades, this approach has the potential to detect secular changes in ice mass, providing a new tool for monitoring ice sheet evolution using seismic methods.
