Seismic Anisotropy

My primary work focuses on measureing azimuthal anisotropy in the upper mantle of the southwest Pacific. Seismic anisotropy, the directional dependence of seismic wave velocity, results from the alignment of olivine crystals due to mantle flow and deformation. By measuring how shear waves split into fast and slow fast components, we can infer past and present dynamics in the lithosphere and asthenosphere.

Traditional SKS splitting methods face challenges in the southwest Pacific due to poor azimuthal coverage and complex anisotropic structure. I developed methods that combine SKS phases with direct S phases from deep earthquakes, using splitting intensity to extract robust measurements from the data. This workflow improved azimuthal coverage and enabled us to obtain first-order single-layer anisotropic measurements within the region. We also explored the possibility of D'' anisotropic contamination at certain stations, and its implications for interpretations of upper mantle anisotropy.

This work is used as constraints for full-waveform azimuthal anisotropy tomography of the region, and is currently in preparation for submission.

Marine Geophysics

Ocean bottom seismometers (OBS) allow us to record seismic waves in poorly sampled oceanic regions. My PhD research will focus on the dataset collected from an ambitious OBS deployment at the Galapagos triple junction, a mid-ocean ridge ~1,200 km West of the Galapagos Islands. We sail in December 2026 to retrieve the seismometers, after which we will apply a number of seismic methods in order to better understand the structure and dynamics of mid-ocean ridges, newly-formed oceanic crust, and the underlying mantle driving these processes.

I plan to specialize in body wave techniques, which mainly uses teleseismic P and S waves generated from global seismicity. This section will be updated as the project progresses!

Antarctic Ice Sheet Structure

During an REU at Brown University, I used Ps receiver functions to study Antarctic ice sheet properties. Seismic waves convert from P to S at sharp velocity contrasts, allowing us to measure internal structure from seismic data. We applied this to shallow crustal structure in Antarctica, focusing on the Antarctic Ice Sheet.

At high frequencies, Ps receiver functions can resolve ice thickness changes of ~200 m and velocity variations of ~5%. Thin low-velocity layers at the base of the ice (possibly liquid water or wet sediments) produce distinctive signatures in the receiver functions.

For stations operating over decades, this approach has the potential to detect secular changes in ice mass, providing a new tool for monitoring ice sheet evolution using seismic methods.
