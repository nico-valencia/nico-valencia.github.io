Seismic Anisotropy & Shear-Wave Splitting

When seismic waves travel through the Earth's mantle, they don't always travel at the same speed in every direction. This anisotropy arises because olivine crystals in the mantle align in response to deformation and flow. By measuring how shear waves split into fast and slow components, we can map the orientation and strength of anisotropy—and infer the patterns of mantle flow that created it.

Azimuthal Anisotropy in the Southwest Pacific

My primary research focuses on measuring azimuthal anisotropy in the upper mantle beneath the Southwest Pacific—a tectonically complex region with multiple subduction zones, back-arc basins, and the Australian continental margin.

Traditional SKS splitting methods struggle here due to poor azimuthal coverage and complex anisotropic structure. I'm developing methods that combine SKS phases with direct S waves from deep earthquakes, using splitting intensity to extract robust measurements even from challenging data.

These measurements will serve as constraints for full-waveform azimuthal anisotropy tomography of the region, helping us understand the coupling between subduction dynamics and upper mantle flow.

Collaborators: Barbara Romanowicz, Marco Malusà, BSL Broadband Team
Presented: AGU Fall Meeting 2024

Multi-Layer Anisotropy Methods

Standard shear-wave splitting methods assume a single layer of anisotropy with a horizontal symmetry axis. But the real Earth often has multiple anisotropic layers—perhaps from different episodes of deformation, or from the lithosphere and asthenosphere contributing different signals.

I've been developing methods to measure and model multi-layer anisotropy using splitting intensity, building on theoretical expressions that describe how SI varies with backazimuth when multiple layers are present. This allows more robust measurements in regions with complex structure.

Collaborators: Barbara Romanowicz
Presented: SSA Annual Meeting 2024

Ocean-Bottom Seismology

Most of Earth's surface is covered by oceans, but most seismometers are on land. This leaves vast regions of the planet poorly sampled. Ocean-bottom seismometers (OBS) allow us to record seismic waves in oceanic regions—but they come with unique challenges: noise from ocean currents, instrument coupling, and limited deployment durations.

PhD Research at UCSB

Beginning in 2026, my PhD research at UC Santa Barbara will focus on ocean-bottom seismology and new OBS datasets. I'll be studying seismic structure beneath oceanic regions, developing methods for OBS data analysis, and working to fill in the gaps in our imaging of Earth's interior.

Computational Seismology

Modern seismology involves processing thousands of waveforms, managing large datasets, and building reproducible analysis workflows. I develop computational tools for seismic data processing, measurement automation, and visualization.

Tools & Approaches

  • Waveform processing: Automated workflows for downloading, preprocessing, and quality-controlling seismic data using ObsPy
  • Splitting measurements: Python implementations of splitting intensity and transverse minimization methods
  • Visualization: Publication-quality figures using Matplotlib, PyGMT, and Cartopy
  • Reproducibility: Version-controlled analysis pipelines designed to be transparent and shareable

Previous Research

Antarctic Ice Sheet Structure from Receiver Functions

During a summer REU at Brown University, I worked on using Ps receiver functions to study the Antarctic ice sheet. Seismic waves convert from P to S at sharp velocity contrasts—including the base of the ice sheet—allowing us to measure ice thickness and internal properties.

At high frequencies, receiver functions can resolve ice thickness changes of ~200 m and velocity variations of ~5%. We also found that thin low-velocity layers at the base of the ice (possibly liquid water or wet sediments) produce distinctive receiver function signatures.

For stations operating over decades, this approach has the potential to detect secular changes in ice mass—a new way to monitor ice sheet evolution using seismic data.

Collaborators: Chengxin Gao, Karen Fischer
Institution: Brown University
Presented: AGU Fall Meeting 2024
