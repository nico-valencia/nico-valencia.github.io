About

I'm a geophysicist interested in understanding the Earth through the waves that travel through it.

In Fall 2026, I'll begin a PhD at UC Santa Barbara, where I'll work on ocean-bottom seismology and use new OBS datasets to study the structure beneath the oceans. I graduated from UC Berkeley in 2025 with a double major in Geophysics and Astrophysics.

My research so far has focused on seismic anisotropy—the way seismic waves travel at different speeds depending on their direction—and what that tells us about how the Earth's mantle flows and deforms. I'm drawn to problems that require both careful observation and computational thinking: processing large datasets, building analysis workflows, and finding signals in noise.

How I got here

I came to geophysics through an interest in how we can learn about things we can't directly see. Seismology appealed to me because it's a kind of remote sensing of the deep Earth—we record vibrations at the surface and work backward to understand structure hundreds of kilometers below.

At Berkeley, I worked in the Global Seismology Research Group on azimuthal anisotropy in the Southwest Pacific, developing methods to measure shear-wave splitting from both SKS and direct S phases. I also spent a summer at Brown University working on receiver functions and Antarctic ice sheet structure—a very different application of seismic methods, but one that showed me how broadly these techniques can be applied.

Beyond the seismograms

Science is central to how I think, but it's not the entirety of what I'm interested in. I like photography, writing, and paying attention to places. I'm interested in how scientific work actually happens—the fieldwork, the instruments, the collaboration, the long hours staring at waveforms.

I started this website partly as a professional portfolio but also as a place to collect things: research, yes, but also field notes, photographs, and writing that doesn't fit neatly into an academic CV.

I believe that the best scientists are curious about more than their immediate research questions. I try to stay curious.
